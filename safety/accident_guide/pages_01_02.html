Critical Window
The Golden Hour
60 Minutes

Act in the first 60 minutes. After that, evidence degrades rapidly — memory fades, stories merge, scenes change and people disperse across shifts.

In the first 60 minutes, gather all of the following:
1
When
Time & Date
Point of incident vs. time reported. Document any delay and the reasons for it.
2
Who
Personnel
Names of all involved, injured, and witnesses. Roles and length of service.
3
Where
Location
Exact location: bay #, aisle, machine ID, GPS if applicable. Environmental conditions.
8
Planned Actions
Next Steps
Further interviews needed, equipment testing, temporary barriers or controls.
What does the person involved think caused the accident?
Rule: Capture their perspective first, without challenge or judgement.
4
What Happened
Events
Factual timeline of events. Zero assumptions — only proven facts.
7
Actions Taken
Immediate
First aid given, CCTV secured, area made safe, initial witness statements.
6
Equipment
Assets
Asset IDs (serial #s), tool conditions, weights, last maintenance date.
5
Result
Impact
Specific injury extent. Property and equipment damage. Operational impact.
Why This Window Matters
Act Before the Evidence Disappears
  • Memory fades fast — especially under stress. Brains also fill in gaps we cannot remember.
  • Stories dilute — witnesses talk and versions merge; one account absorbs another without anyone intending it.
  • Blame deflects early — timelines can shift in the first 15–30 minutes as people re-adjust their account.
  • Scenes change — floors get cleaned, equipment is moved, areas are reset.
  • People disperse — coordinating across different shifts later is a significant challenge.
  • CCTV auto-deletes — footage often overwrites within 24–72 hours. Securing it cannot wait.
Do this. In this order.
First Response
1
Welfare of the injured person First aid, medical assessment, hospital if needed. Always step one.
2
Make the area safe Isolate hazards. Protect the dignity of anyone injured on the floor — ask others to leave, screen the area.
3
Secure CCTV immediately Identify and hold footage before it overwrites. Don't delay on this.
4
Separate witnesses now Before they speak to each other — stories align very quickly once people compare notes.
5
Photograph the scene Multiple angles. Before anything is moved, cleaned or repaired.
6
Take statements individually Two questions for each person — see below.

Statements & Assumptions
Manager's Quick Reference Guide
02
"What do you think caused this to happen?"
Ask every person involved — including the injured operative. This is your most powerful question.
Q1

What happened?
Facts only. What they saw — or didn't see. No interpretation, no guessing. This is their factual recollection.

Q2

What do you think caused it?
Now you invite their opinion. Kept separate, this removes assumption from the factual account. Nine times out of ten, people will tell you exactly what the problem was.

Evidence That Holds Up
Taking Witness Statements
Interview separately

Always one-to-one. Take them off the floor. Use a translator if needed. Make them comfortable — not on trial. Ask open questions: "Tell me what you saw" not "Do you think the driver was going too fast?"

Bullet points are fine

Not everyone can write fluently. Help them — take notes as they speak if needed. Ask them to read it back and confirm it reflects what they said. Then get it signed.

Every statement must have:  Full name  ·  Date and time  ·  Signature — without all three it cannot be relied on if disputed later by the injured party, a claim, an EHO or an external health authority.
Before You Draw Conclusions
Manage Your Assumptions

We all have biases — a person's history, a known busy shift, a team you know well. They shape conclusions quietly. Write your assumptions down, then challenge each one.

Have I treated every person involved as equal?

Did I arrive with pre-judgments about who or what was at fault?

Am I stopping at the first explanation that fits?

Have I checked what should be happening vs. what is happening day-to-day?

Could this same thing happen again right now in this area?

Have I asked for help if I'm not sure? That's always the right call.

03
Root Cause Thinking
Attack the Process, Not the Operative

"Human error" is not a root cause — it's where the investigation starts. Ask why the error was possible. Walk the area with fresh eyes: is this how the task is supposed to run? Consider all six factors before forming a conclusion.

People
Were others working unsafely nearby? Peer pressure to move faster? Was supervision present? Any prior behaviour or known issues?
Equipment
Pre-use check completed? Any prior defect reports? Was the right tool being used? Maintenance up to date? Any damage?
Environment
Lighting, floor condition, congestion, layout. Unusually busy? Operational pressures driving faster work?
Method & Process
Is there a WBI? Was it being followed? Are shortcuts normalised in this area? Does the standard match how the job is actually done?
Training
Trained for this specific task? Training recent and relevant? Does it reflect current practice? Check the records — don't assume.
Systems & Standards
Are KPIs or targets creating pressure to skip steps? Is the risk assessment current? Has this hazard been flagged before?
The fresh-eyes test: If someone started in this role today, based on what you now know — would the same thing happen again? If yes, that's where your action needs to be. Start in the immediate area, then ask: is this a problem for just this bay? This department? The whole site?
What We Know 100%
Hard Evidence Checklist
CCTV footage — identified, secured, reviewed
Scene photographs — taken before anything moved
Measurements & physical evidence — marks, damage, layout
WBI / SOP — what should have been happening
Training records — for the specific task performed
Equipment records — asset ID, maintenance, defect history
Signed witness statements — name, date, signature
The operative's own account — what they believe caused it

If accounts differ significantly, be honest: "Based on the available evidence, we believe..." is a legitimate finding. Don't claim certainty you don't have.

Your Quality Check
The WBR Test
"Could I present this at today's WBR to the site leader and their manager?"
What happened, when and where
What the operative said caused it
Root causes identified
Actions taken immediately
Actions planned to prevent recurrence
Current status and condition of the operative

If you can walk through that list confidently, your initial investigation is done. If not — you know exactly what's still missing.

Preventing Recurrence — Think Wide
Corrective Actions
1
Eliminate the hazardRemove the risk entirely — redesign the task or process
2
Engineering controlPhysical fix — guard, interlock, layout change. No behaviour required.
3
Process or training changeUpdated WBI, refreshed training, supervision standard
4
Communication & awarenessBrief the team — use the incident (no names) as a learning moment
5
PPE (last resort only)Never the primary fix for a significant hazard

You don't need to solve every problem yourself. Bring your ideas to your manager and the safety team — that's how changes happen.

Reporting Obligations — Know When to Flag
LTI Awareness
🇬🇧 UK — RIDDOR

Any absence from work resulting from a workplace injury must be reported to the HSE under RIDDOR. Inform the safety team immediately — do not wait to see if the absence extends.

🇩🇪 Germany — BG Reporting

Lost time injuries must be reported to the Berufsgenossenschaft. Failure to report is a compliance breach. Inform the safety team on the day of the incident.

Watch for delayed LTIs: An operative may complete their shift but not return the following day due to the same injury — pain often worsens overnight. If someone has a workplace accident and then calls in sick, even days later, that is a potential LTI. Tell the safety team immediately. Don't wait to be certain.
