Manager's Quick Reference Guide
01
Critical Window
The Golden Hour
60 Minutes

Act in the first 60 minutes. After that, evidence degrades rapidly — memory fades, stories merge, scenes change and people disperse across shifts.

In the first 60 minutes, gather all of the following:
1
When
Time & Date
Point of incident vs. time reported. Document any delay and the reasons for it.
2
Who
Personnel
Names of all involved, injured, and witnesses. Roles and length of service.
3
Where
Location
Exact location: bay #, aisle, machine ID, GPS if applicable. Environmental conditions.
8
Planned Actions
Next Steps
Further interviews needed, equipment testing, temporary barriers or controls.
What does the person involved think caused the accident?
Rule: Capture their perspective first, without challenge or judgement.
4
What Happened
Events
Factual timeline of events. Zero assumptions — only proven facts.
7
Actions Taken
Immediate
First aid given, CCTV secured, area made safe, initial witness statements.
6
Equipment
Assets
Asset IDs (serial #s), tool conditions, weights, last maintenance date.
5
Result
Impact
Specific injury extent. Property and equipment damage. Operational impact.
Why This Window Matters
Act Before the Evidence Disappears
  • Memory fades fast — especially under stress. Brains also fill in gaps we cannot remember.
  • Stories dilute — witnesses talk and versions merge; one account absorbs another without anyone intending it.
  • Blame deflects early — timelines can shift in the first 15–30 minutes as people re-adjust their account.
  • Scenes change — floors get cleaned, equipment is moved, areas are reset.
  • People disperse — coordinating across different shifts later is a significant challenge.
  • CCTV auto-deletes — footage often overwrites within 24–72 hours. Securing it cannot wait.
Do this. In this order.
First Response
1
Welfare of the injured person First aid, medical assessment, hospital if needed. Always step one.
2
Make the area safe Isolate hazards. Protect the dignity of anyone injured on the floor — ask others to leave, screen the area.
3
Secure CCTV immediately Identify and hold footage before it overwrites. Don't delay on this.
4
Separate witnesses now Before they speak to each other — stories align very quickly once people compare notes.
5
Photograph the scene Multiple angles. Before anything is moved, cleaned or repaired.
6
Take statements individually Two questions for each person — see below.

Statements & Assumptions
Manager's Quick Reference Guide
02
"What do you think caused this to happen?"
Ask every person involved — including the injured operative. This is your most powerful question.
Q1

What happened?
Facts only. What they saw — or didn't see. No interpretation, no guessing. This is their factual recollection.

Q2

What do you think caused it?
Now you invite their opinion. Kept separate, this removes assumption from the factual account. Nine times out of ten, people will tell you exactly what the problem was.

Evidence That Holds Up
Taking Witness Statements
Interview separately

Always one-to-one. Take them off the floor. Use a translator if needed. Make them comfortable — not on trial. Ask open questions: "Tell me what you saw" not "Do you think the driver was going too fast?"

Bullet points are fine

Not everyone can write fluently. Help them — take notes as they speak if needed. Ask them to read it back and confirm it reflects what they said. Then get it signed.

Every statement must have:  Full name  ·  Date and time  ·  Signature — without all three it cannot be relied on if disputed later by the injured party, a claim, an EHO or an external health authority.
Before You Draw Conclusions
Manage Your Assumptions

We all have biases — a person's history, a known busy shift, a team you know well. They shape conclusions quietly. Write your assumptions down, then challenge each one.

Have I treated every person involved as equal?

Did I arrive with pre-judgments about who or what was at fault?

Am I stopping at the first explanation that fits?

Have I checked what should be happening vs. what is happening day-to-day?

Could this same thing happen again right now in this area?

Have I asked for help if I'm not sure? That's always the right call.

03
Root Cause Thinking
Attack the Process, Not the Operative

"Human error" is not a root cause — it's where the investigation starts. Ask why the error was possible. Walk the area with fresh eyes: is this how the task is supposed to run? Consider all six factors before forming a conclusion.

People
Were others working unsafely nearby? Peer pressure to move faster? Was supervision present? Any prior behaviour or known issues?
Equipment
Pre-use check completed? Any prior defect reports? Was the right tool being used? Maintenance up to date? Any damage?
Environment
Lighting, floor condition, congestion, layout. Unusually busy? Operational pressures driving faster work?
Method & Process
Is there a WBI? Was it being followed? Are shortcuts normalised in this area? Does the standard match how the job is actually done?
Training
Trained for this specific task? Training recent and relevant? Does it reflect current practice? Check the records — don't assume.
Systems & Standards
Are KPIs or targets creating pressure to skip steps? Is the risk assessment current? Has this hazard been flagged before?
The fresh-eyes test: If someone started in this role today, based on what you now know — would the same thing happen again? If yes, that's where your action needs to be. Start in the immediate area, then ask: is this a problem for just this bay? This department? The whole site?
What We Know 100%
Hard Evidence Checklist
CCTV footage — identified, secured, reviewed
Scene photographs — taken before anything moved
Measurements & physical evidence — marks, damage, layout
WBI / SOP — what should have been happening
Training records — for the specific task performed
Equipment records — asset ID, maintenance, defect history
Signed witness statements — name, date, signature
The operative's own account — what they believe caused it

If accounts differ significantly, be honest: "Based on the available evidence, we believe..." is a legitimate finding. Don't claim certainty you don't have.

Your Quality Check
The WBR Test
"Could I present this at today's WBR to the site leader and their manager?"
What happened, when and where
What the operative said caused it
Root causes identified
Actions taken immediately
Actions planned to prevent recurrence
Current status and condition of the operative

If you can walk through that list confidently, your initial investigation is done. If not — you know exactly what's still missing.

Accident Investigation — Manager's Quick Reference Guide
04
Preventing Recurrence — Think Wide
Corrective Actions
1
Eliminate the hazardRemove the risk entirely — redesign the task or process
2
Engineering controlPhysical fix — guard, interlock, layout change. No behaviour required.
3
Process or training changeUpdated WBI, refreshed training, supervision standard
4
Communication & awarenessBrief the team — use the incident (no names) as a learning moment
5
PPE (last resort only)Never the primary fix for a significant hazard

You don't need to solve every problem yourself. Bring your ideas to your manager and the safety team — that's how changes happen.

Reporting Obligations — Know When to Flag
LTI Awareness
🇬🇧 UK — RIDDOR

Any absence from work resulting from a workplace injury must be reported to the HSE under RIDDOR. Inform the safety team immediately — do not wait to see if the absence extends.

🇩🇪 Germany — BG Reporting

Lost time injuries must be reported to the Berufsgenossenschaft. Failure to report is a compliance breach. Inform the safety team on the day of the incident.

Watch for delayed LTIs: An operative may complete their shift but not return the following day due to the same injury — pain often worsens overnight. If someone has a workplace accident and then calls in sick, even days later, that is a potential LTI. Tell the safety team immediately. Don't wait to be certain.

Injury Guide 1 of 5 — Pain, strain or sprain reported
05
Use with pages 01–04 Welfare first, make it safe, secure CCTV, separate witnesses, then ask "What do you think caused this?"
Someone reports pain
Ask and note in the first 60 minutes
1
Onset
How it started
Did the pain start and get worse, or just appear? What were they handling at that exact moment?
2
Hands
Small items
Handling small items: both hands or just one? One-handed picking overloads the same arm, shoulder and back.
3
Body Position
Posture
Facing the load and turning with the feet, or twisting at the waist? Twisting is a key factor in back pain.
4
History
Same area
Any problem in the same area before? When, what happened, was it reported? Ask without judgement.
What have they been lifting or handling over the last 60 minutes?
Rule: Go back the full hour. The cause is often not the last lift.
5
The Load
What was handled
Weight, size, shape, grip. Awkward or unstable? Was the weight known or labelled?
6
Heights & Reach
Where
Picked up and put down at floor, waist, shoulder or overhead height? Any stooping or stretching?
7
Pace & Repetition
How often
Lifts per hour, targets, rotation, breaks taken. Start or end of shift: was fatigue a factor?
8
Aids & Help
Support
Was a trolley, tilter or second person available? Trained and following the WBI?
What We Know 100%
Evidence to Secure
CCTV of the full hour — how they lifted, carried and turned
Weights handled — weigh a sample, don't trust the label
Pick-up and put-down heights — measured and photographed
WBI and training record — current, task-specific, matches the job
Task data — units per hour, rotation plan, breaks taken
Pain score log — number and time, re-asked through the shift
Look Wider — Not Just the Person
Contributing Factors
People

Rushing? Skipping the team lift? Is the same technique normal for others here?

Equipment

Trolleys, tilters or lift aids available, working and within reach?

Environment

Cold stiffens muscles. Work height, floor, congestion, space to turn the feet.

Method

Does the WBI match the real task? Can the layout remove the twist or reach?

Training

Recent and task-specific? Do they know to turn their feet, not their waist?

Systems

Do targets drive the pace? Is job rotation in place? Similar reports here?

"On a scale of 1 to 10, how bad is the pain right now?"
Write down the number and the time. Ask again later — a rising number means it is getting worse.

Slip, Trip & Fall
Injury Guide 2 of 5 — Slip, trip or fall on the same level or from height
06
Use with pages 01–04 Welfare first, make it safe, secure CCTV, separate witnesses, then ask "What do you think caused this?"
Someone slips, trips or falls
Ask and note in the first 60 minutes
1
Slip
Loss of grip
Wet, oily, dusty, icy, condensation or film? Where did the foot lose grip? When was it last cleaned?
2
Trip
Foot caught
What did the foot catch on: pallet, wrap, cable, step, threshold, damaged floor or loose stock?
3
Fall
Height & landing
Same level, or from steps, ladder, platform, dock edge or vehicle? How far? What did they land on?
4
Footwear
Grip
What were they wearing? Soles worn? Right type for wet or chilled areas? Photograph the soles.
What did they slip on, trip over or fall from — and had it been there before?
Rule: Make it safe, then photograph before it is cleaned or moved.
5
Carrying
Hands & view
Carrying, pushing or pulling? Could they see their feet and the route? A hand free for the rail?
6
Attention & Pace
Focus
Rushing? On a phone or wearing headphones? Looking where they were going? No blame.
7
Lighting & View
Visibility
Light levels, glare, shadow, moving from bright to dark. Was the hazard marked or visible?
8
Health
Before the fall
Dizzy, unwell or unsteady beforehand? Did they hit their head or black out, even briefly?
What We Know 100%
Evidence to Secure
Photos of the surface or obstruction — wide and close-up, with scale
Footwear — photos of soles, policy and what was issued
Cleaning and inspection records — last clean, spill reports
Floor, steps or ladder — damage, gradient, inspection tag, handrail
CCTV — direction, pace, what they carried, the lead-up
Lighting and weather — light levels; wet or ice brought in
Look Wider — Not Just the Person
Contributing Factors
People

Shortcuts across wet or cluttered areas? Are others working round the same hazard?

Equipment

Right steps or ladder for the job? Handrails, edge protection, non-slip treads?

Environment

Floor finish, leaks, condensation, chiller doors, weather brought in, lighting.

Method

Spill procedure: who cleans, how fast, cordon and signs? Clean-as-you-go in place?

Training

Footwear rules, safe use of steps, reporting spills and near misses.

Systems

Housekeeping standard and inspection frequency. Any trend for this location?

MHE Collision
Injury Guide 3 of 5 — Forklift, reach truck or pallet truck collision
07
Use with pages 01–04 Welfare first, make it safe, secure CCTV, separate witnesses, then ask "What do you think caused this?"
Material handling equipment hits a person, truck or structure
Ask and note in the first 60 minutes
1
Who & What
People and asset
Driver, pedestrian or second truck. Truck type and asset ID. Is the driver authorised on this truck?
2
Direction & Speed
Movement
Forward or reversing? Speed? Turning or at a junction? Was the horn or warning used?
3
Load & View
Visibility
What was carried, at what fork height? Could the driver see past the load? Should they have been reversing?
4
Pedestrian
Why there
Why were they there? In a walkway or crossing? Phone or headphones? Did they see the truck?
Were the truck and the pedestrian ever meant to be in the same place at the same time?
Rule: Take the truck out of use, keep the key, preserve the truck data.
5
Layout
Traffic routes
Segregation, barriers, crossing points, blind corners, mirrors, speed limits, floor marking.
6
Truck Condition
Pre-use check
Check done? Brakes, horn, lights, beacon or blue spot, seat belt. Any earlier defects?
7
Driver Condition
Fit to drive
Alert and fit? Hours on the truck, breaks, pace and targets. Post-incident checks per policy.
8
Damage
Property
Racking, doors, stock or other trucks hit? Damaged racking must be inspected before use.
What We Know 100%
Evidence to Secure
Truck isolated — key removed, tagged out until inspected
Truck data — telematics, impact, speed, key or PIN log
CCTV from several angles — approach, impact and aftermath
Scene photos and measurements — positions, sight lines, marks
Pre-use check and service history — that shift, that truck
Driver authorisation — training on this truck type, refresher
Look Wider — Not Just the Person
Contributing Factors
People

Pedestrians using truck routes as a shortcut? Horn used at junctions? What is normal here?

Equipment

Blue spots, beacons, mirrors, collision detection, speed limiters fitted and working?

Environment

Congestion, blind corners, noise, lighting, floor condition, peak periods.

Method

Does the traffic plan (segregation, crossings, priority) match how the site runs?

Training

Authorised on this truck type? Refresher current? Pedestrian awareness covered?

Systems

Do targets drive speed? Are MHE near misses here reported and reviewed?

Injury Guide 4 of 5 — Roll cage, trolley or tugger cart movement resulting in a collision
08
Use with pages 01–04 Welfare first, make it safe, secure CCTV, separate witnesses, then ask "What do you think caused this?"
A moving cart or cage hits, traps or crushes
Ask and note in the first 60 minutes
1
What Moved
Equipment
Roll cage, trolley, tugger cart or stacked pallet? Pushed or pulled? By how many people?
2
The Load
Weight and view
Weight and height. Stable and secured? Overloaded? Could they see over or round it?
3
Wheels & Brakes
Condition
Castors turning freely, or damaged and seized? Brakes working? Which wheels led?
4
Route & Floor
Surface
Ramp, slope, dock plate, threshold, damaged or dirty floor? Did the cage run away?
What did the cage hit or trap — and was it under control at the time?
Rule: Tag the cage out before it goes back into circulation.
5
Hands & Body
Contact
Where were hands and feet? Trapped between cage and rack, wall, door or another cage? Foot run over?
6
Speed & Pace
Movement
How fast? Rushing, targets, peak-time congestion? Two-way traffic in the same aisle?
7
Visibility
Crossings
Blind corners, doorways, crossing points, mirrors. Could they see the person, and the person them?
8
Parking
At rest
Parked with brakes on, out of the walkway, not on a slope? Did it move by itself?
What We Know 100%
Evidence to Secure
Cage or cart tagged out — asset number; wheels and brakes photographed
Load weight and loading — measure it; photograph how it was loaded
Route and floor — gradient, ramps, thresholds, dock plates
CCTV — speed, direction, who was pushing, their view
Inspection and maintenance records — plus defect reports
Cage handling rules — max load and height, push not pull, WBI
Look Wider — Not Just the Person
Contributing Factors
People

Pulling cages backwards or overloading to save trips? Are others doing the same?

Equipment

Wheel type suited to the floor? Brakes fitted? Does defect reporting work?

Environment

Ramps, thresholds, narrow aisles, congestion, worn floor, lighting.

Method

Max load and height, route, push or pull, right of way, parking rules.

Training

Cage handling and pushing technique. Pre-use inspection covered?

Systems

Targets that reward speed? Maintenance schedule? Near misses reported?

Injury Guide 5 of 5 — Someone has bumped or walked into something
09
Use with pages 01–04 Welfare first, make it safe, secure CCTV, separate witnesses, then ask "What do you think caused this?"
Someone bumps into a fixed or protruding object
Ask and note in the first 60 minutes
1
What Was Hit
The object
Racking beam, pallet corner, forks, door, guard, cage, low structure or sharp edge? Fixed, or moved there?
2
Body Part
Force
Which part of the body, and how hard? Head, face or eyes need a medical check even if they look minor.
3
Movement
Direction
Walking, turning, bending or reaching? Forwards, backwards or sideways? How fast?
4
Attention
Focus
Looking where they were going? Carrying, on a phone, wearing headphones, talking? No blame.
What did they hit — and would the next person hit it too?
Rule: If the hazard is still there, guard or mark it now.
5
Route
Path taken
Normal route or a shortcut? Was the route blocked, forcing them through a gap?
6
Should It Be There?
Object status
Left out, moved or protruding? Temporary or permanent? Has anyone hit it before?
7
Visibility
Lighting
Lighting, glare, colour or contrast marking, warning signs. Could they see the hazard at all?
8
Space
Housekeeping
Clearance height and width. Stock stored in the aisle? Was the area congested or busy?

Evidence to Secure
Photos of the object — position and clearance; measure it
View from their eye line — photograph along their direction of travel
CCTV — route, pace, what they carried, who left it there

Housekeeping and inspection records — when last checked
Near-miss log — earlier bumps at this location

Contributing Factors
People

Regular route or shortcut? Is weaving round hazards seen as normal here?

Equipment

Rack protectors, guarding, padding, edge protection or mirrors fitted?

Environment

Lighting, contrast marking, congestion, narrow gaps, low structure.

Method

Are pallets, forks or cages left standing in walkways? Where should they go?

Training

Aware of the hazard zone? Site layout covered in induction?

Systems

Hazard reporting, inspection frequency, previous bumps, layout change control.
